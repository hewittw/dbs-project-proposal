Your own
tax calendar

One line · the problem and the user

Problem: every business has its own tax deadlines depending on what it does, and there's no centralized, cheap, intuitive way to see yours. User: businesses in general, especially smaller ones without someone tracking every date for them.

Every business has its own tax calendar. Which deadlines apply depends on what you do: your entity type, state, industry, payroll, contractors and more. It's so specific that free calendars just list every date for everyone. This asks a few questions about your business, then keeps your own deadlines up to date in the calendar app you already use.

04How it works

Answer a few questions. Get your dates.

Tell it who you are, and it builds a calendar of the deadlines that apply to you, plus a countdown to the next one. Dates that land on a weekend move to the next business day.

    05Interoperability

    Connects to the tools you already use

    The calendar gets much better when it doesn't depend on someone typing answers. With connections to QuickBooks and other Intuit products, Salesforce, and an MCP server of its own, the same deadlines can reach everyone who touches a business's taxes.

    Business owners

    Auto-filled from your books

    Connect QuickBooks and the calendar reads what it needs: entity type, whether you run payroll, and whether you pay contractors. Your dates appear without filling out a form, and they update when your business changes.

    Bookkeepers

    Deadlines next to the books

    Upcoming deadlines show up as tasks and reminders where bookkeepers already work, so a 941 or 1099 date never surprises anyone. One view across all the businesses they keep books for.

    Accountants

    Every client, one calendar

    Pull clients from Salesforce or other Intuit tools and see every client's deadlines in one place. This answers what Mom's colleagues said: there's no centralized system, and nothing cheap and easy.

    • ConnectQuickBooks Online + IntuitThe QuickBooks Online API (OAuth 2.0) supplies the facts that decide which deadlines apply. Intuit also publishes an official QuickBooks MCP server (preview).
    • ConnectSalesforceFirms that track clients in a CRM can sync client records in and push deadlines back out as tasks.
    • MCPTax Calendar MCP serverAny AI assistant can ask "what's due for this business next month?" or "which clients have a 1099 deadline this week?" and get answers from the same rules table, with the source link.

    When: after v1. v1 proves the rules and the calendar with manual answers. A QuickBooks connection is the first stretch for v2, and the multi-client accountant view with Salesforce comes after the fair.

    06Holistic / Design / Systems / AI

    Focus

    SystemsRecommended

    The hard part isn't the screen, it's keeping the rules right. Deadlines are formulas that change every year, move when they land on weekends and holidays, and get postponed county by county after disasters. Going deep on Systems means a versioned rules table with a source for every row, a daily background job that sends reminders and flags rule changes, and a calendar feed that updates itself. Because the rules keep changing, they live as data that can be updated, not as hard-coded logic.

    Why not AI: a wrong date from a model is a real penalty for someone. Why not Design: the intake is only five questions. The trust comes from the data being right.

    09Open questions

    Risks

    • R1
      Accuracy and liability. What happens if a deadline is wrong? Every date links to its official source with a "last verified" date. "Not tax advice" goes on the page, in emails, and in every feed event. Tests check the engine against Pub 509.
    • R2
      How to cover businesses in general when every one is so specific. Start with the most common federal + Illinois rules, then add rules as data. Mom's clients are the most realistic first testers.
    • R3
      Where the rules data comes from, and how it stays current. No official machine-readable feed: the IRS and Illinois publish HTML and PDFs. Rules are entered by hand, so v1 stays small. The IRS posts next year's Pub 509 draft early, so I can review changes once a year.
    • R4
      Disaster extensions are by county and come out with no schedule, and there's no IRS RSS feed. v1: I add them by hand. v2: a watcher using FEMA's open data flags them for my review.
    • R5
      Collective already offers a free subscribable federal calendar. My edge has to be personal dates, state deadlines, and change alerts.
    • R6
      Free-tier limits. Resend allows 100 emails a day, Supabase pauses idle projects, and Google Calendar refreshes feeds slowly.
    • R7
      Connecting to financial data. QuickBooks and Salesforce access means OAuth, app review, and handling sensitive books. Read-only access to the few fields that decide deadlines, nothing more.
